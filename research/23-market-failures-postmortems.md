# Dead & Pivoted Event-Discovery Startups — Postmortem Patterns

> Fifteen years of event-discovery corpses encode exactly which failure modes our autonomous concierge design does and does not neutralize — this dossier is the empirical check on our core product bet.

## Landscape inventory

### Consumer social/discovery apps — dead

- **IRL (Get Together Inc.)** (dead 2023; https://techcrunch.com/2023/06/26/irl-shut-down-fake-users/) — Gen-Z event-organizing / social calendar app pitched as the Facebook Events replacement; raised to unicorn status on claimed 20M MAU. $200M raised total; $170M SoftBank-led Series C at $1.17B valuation (2021); shut down June 27, 2023 after board investigation found 95% of users were bots; SEC charged founder Abraham Shafi with $170M fraud (July 2024), DOJ added wire/securities fraud and obstruction charges. Free, never monetized. Relevance: the category's frequency/retention problem is so severe that its best-funded consumer play faked engagement rather than admit it — real organic demand for a general "events social layer" at consumer-social scale did not exist. Warns against DAU-shaped goals.
- **Plancast** (dead 2012; https://techcrunch.com/2012/01/22/post-mortem-for-plancast/) — Social event-plan sharing ("Foursquare for the future"). ~$800K raised; wound down Jan 2012; founder Mark Hendrickson's postmortem is the category's canonical text. Free. Relevance: names the root cause we must engineer around — plans are LOW-FREQUENCY content, no daily/weekly habit forms; early-adopter traction misleads; many events are private and unsuitable for broadcast.
- **Hugecity** (dead ~2015; http://www.hughmalkin.com/blogwriter/2015/9/23/why-no-one-has-solved-event-discovery) — Facebook-graph-powered local event discovery (events friends were attending); founder Hugh Malkin wrote the definitive aggregator-economics essay. Grew to ~1M visitors/month; died on retention + Facebook API restrictions. Free/ads. Relevance: essay quantifies the structural traps — low usage frequency, spiky demand (50% of tickets at announce, 50% week-of), event value decays with distance, promoters prefer Facebook's cheaper targeting, thin affiliate economics.

### Curated concierge/booking apps — dead or fire-sold

- **YPlan** (acquired 2016, app killed 2017; https://techcrunch.com/2016/10/21/time-out-for-yplan/) — London "tonight/this week" curated events booking app (2012); pivoted with major layoffs end-2014. Raised ~$38M; pre-tax loss £6.2M in last filed year; sold to Time Out Oct 2016 for ~£1.6M in stock — roughly 5 cents on the invested dollar; app discontinued without notice Aug 2017. Commission on bookings. Relevance: the canonical curated-discovery+booking failure — multi-city expansion burned cash, ticket take-rate never covered supply-curation cost. Same "book it for you tonight" promise as our concierge, but with a destination-app surface and affiliate economics.
- **Sosh** (dead 2015 via acquihire; https://techcrunch.com/2015/11/05/postmates-acquihires-activity-concierge-sosh-in-a-fire-sale/) — SF curated "things to do" concierge (2010). $16.3M raised (Khosla, Battery); ran out of money; Postmates acquihired 18 of 40 employees Nov 2015, explicitly buying NO technology; told merchants to move to Eventbrite. Booking commissions. Relevance: high-quality curation and "concierge" branding did not save it — curation labor per city doesn't amortize; our agentic pipeline must make per-user curation near-zero marginal cost or it recreates Sosh.
- **Dojo (London)** (dead 2018; https://techcrunch.com/2015/01/26/dojo-funding/) — Curated city-discovery app for London/Paris (2014), backed by Innocent founders, DeepMind founders, Boris Johnson's LCIF. £800K raised (2015); wound down gradually, never acquired despite hoping for a buyer. Free; sponsorship/affiliate. Relevance: even beloved, well-connected, editorially excellent city guides die — taste alone has no business model. Differentiation must be in the closed loop (RSVP + calendar), not recommendation quality.
- **WillCall** (acquired 2014, app sunset 2016; https://techcrunch.com/2014/08/21/concert-giant-ticketfly-acquires-last-minute-ticket-app-willcall/) — Curated last-minute concert list + one-tap ticket purchase + in-venue payments (BarTab), SF/LA/NY. YC-backed, ~$1.2M raised; acquired by Ticketfly Aug 2014; standalone app killed Mar 2016. Relevance: one-tap frictionless checkout was genuinely loved — but only worked where WillCall had direct venue supply deals, capping it at 3 cities. Our one-tap handoff sidesteps per-venue deals by keeping the transaction on the source platform.
- **Jukely** (zombie, nominally active 2026; https://techcrunch.com/2014/11/14/jukely-offers-unlimited-concerts-for-25-a-month/) — $25/mo "unlimited" surplus-inventory concert subscription (2014, NYC). $12.8M raised; expanded to 15+ cities 2015-16; no funding since 2015, no press since ~2019; effectively invisible post-COVID. Relevance: proof a flat events subscription CAN clear ~$25/mo willingness-to-pay — but its inventory was distressed tickets, so quality degraded and the model stalled at niche scale. Our subscription must price the LABOR (concierge automation), not the inventory.

### Aggregators and platform surfaces — dead or dark

- **Zvents** (acquired 2011, shut ~2014-15; https://www.ticketnews.com/2011/12/stubhub-buys-local-events-site-zvents/) — Largest US local-events aggregator/search of its era; SEO + media-partner syndication. 3M+ events/year from 140K marketers; 300+ newspaper partner sites totaling 14M uniques/month; acquired by eBay/StubHub Dec 2011; quietly shut down. Ads / enhanced listings. Relevance: the maximal "aggregate everything" outcome — category-leading coverage yielded only a modest ads-business exit, then a shutdown. Coverage breadth is not a moat and not a business.
- **Yahoo Upcoming (Upcoming.org)** (dead 2013; https://waxy.org/2013/04/the_death_of_upcomingorg/) — Community-driven social events calendar (2003, Yahoo-acquired 2005); Kickstarter-funded indie revival also faded. Yahoo shut it with 11 days' notice, deleting the archive. Relevance: crowdsourced event supply decays without paid incentive; corporate parents starve event products. Also a data-portability cautionary tale — users' event history should be exportable.
- **Facebook Events** (declined as discovery surface, 2018→; https://gfsc.studio/blog/2022/rise-fall-facebook-events/) — Was the de-facto event graph (~100M daily Events users at 2016 peak). Events API restricted April 2018 post-Cambridge Analytica; average Page organic reach eroded across a decade from ~16% (2012) to ~1-2%; organizer "mass exodus"; LinkedIn overtook Facebook as organizers' top promotion channel by 2024. Relevance: (1) the richest event corpus on earth is API-dark — permanently capping any "full coverage" claim; (2) its decline created the fragmentation (Luma/Partiful/Discord/Instagram bios) that makes a cross-source concierge newly valuable — the gap our product exists in.
- **Songkick** (pivoted/dismembered 2017-18; brand alive under Suno 2025; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songkick) — Concert discovery via artist-tracking (scan music library, alert on tour dates); later moved into ticketing. Sued Live Nation/Ticketmaster (2015-2017); discovery app sold to Warner Music July 2017 for ~$5M; $110M Ticketmaster settlement Jan 2018 with Live Nation taking remaining ticketing IP; WMG sold Songkick to Suno Nov 2025. Free; affiliate. Relevance: (1) artist-tracking — a standing subscription to your taste — gave real retention, the only discovery mechanic in the graveyard that worked, and the closest analog to our standing NL preference profile; (2) crossing from discovery into transactions put it in Ticketmaster's kill zone.

### Survivors and consolidators

- **Fever** (active — THE survivor; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fever_(app)) — Madrid discovery app (2011) that pivoted into producing its own "Fever Originals" (Candlelight concerts, immersive exhibits) chosen via its data engine. Unicorn Jan 2022 (Goldman-led $227M); ~$100M raise mid-2025; acquired DICE 2025; Candlelight has sold millions of tickets in 100+ cities; Originals are the main economic engine. Relevance: the category's only breakout escaped aggregation entirely — used discovery data to decide what to MANUFACTURE, capturing full margin and the audience relationship. Discovery-as-front-end only pays when you own something downstream; for us the owned asset is the user's automated workflow + calendar, not inventory.
- **DICE** (acquired by Fever 2025; https://techcrunch.com/2023/08/23/dice-books-65m-for-its-event-discovery-and-ticketing-platform/) — Mobile ticketing + personalized concert discovery; anti-tout, fee-transparent. $122M Series C (2021), $65M (2023); 2022: $28.5M revenue (+359%) but $51.1M operating loss; 2023 restructuring; recommendations drove nearly half of ticket sales. Relevance: best-in-class personalized discovery measurably sells tickets yet STILL couldn't reach standalone profitability on booking fees. Ranking quality is necessary but not sufficient.
- **Fatsoma** (active; https://www.fatsoma.com/) — UK ticketing + promoter-network platform (Manchester, 2006), strong in student nightlife; discovery feed exists but the business is ticketing rails and promoter tools. ~20-year survivor, no unicorn economics. Booking fees. Relevance: survivor pattern — never depended on discovery revenue; discovery is a feature bolted onto rails.
- **Meetup** (active, 3rd owner in 7 years — Bending Spoons 2024; https://techstartups.com/2024/01/11/meetup-acquired-by-milan-based-startup-bending-spoons/) — Recurring interest-group events platform, 60M members; the single most important RSVP source for our launch scope. WeWork acquired 2017 (~$156M reported); dumped at a loss to AlleyCorp 2020; Bending Spoons Jan 2024 — moved ops to Europe, laid off most US staff, tripled organizer fees from June 2024, paywalled features; documented organizer exodus. Relevance: direct supply risk for our launch wedge; the Meetup connector must be treated as a depreciating asset with a migration plan.
- **Eventbrite** (pivoted, taken private Mar 2026; https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20251202408560/en/) — Self-serve ticketing incumbent that spent 2022-2025 trying to become a consumer discovery marketplace. IPO'd 2018 (~$1.76B); $3.5B gross ticket sales 2023; organizer listing fees (2023) drove creator attrition, eliminated 2025 in favor of an ads product (+38% YoY); stock fell to $4.50/share — Bending Spoons closed a ~$500M all-cash take-private Mar 10, 2026. Relevance: even the platform that OWNS inventory and checkout failed to make consumer discovery a growth story — and taxing organizers for discovery placement backfired. Our Eventbrite connector now depends on a cost-cutting owner known for API monetization.
- **Time Out Group (YPlan's buyer)** (pivoted; https://skift.com/2016/10/24/event-booking-app-yplan-raised-38-million-but-was-sold-for-just-3-million/) — City media brand that absorbed YPlan for an "e-commerce transition," then found real growth in physical Time Out Markets (owned food-hall inventory). YPlan app killed within 10 months. Relevance: same lesson as Fever from the media side — events-adjacent winners converged on OWNED physical inventory, abandoning the aggregation layer.
- **Oh My Rockness** (active, shoestring; https://www.ohmyrockness.com/) — Hand-curated NYC/LA/Chicago indie-show calendar since 2004, run by two people; 22 years alive but appealing for donations in 2026. Relevance: niche editorial calendars persist forever at lifestyle scale — complements (scrapable/partnerable supply), not competitors; vertical taste communities outlive horizontal aggregators.
- **Bending Spoons roll-up** (active acquirer — market structure, not a startup; https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/05/what-is-bending-spoons-everything-to-know-about-aols-acquirer/) — Milan cost-cutting operator that owns BOTH Meetup (Jan 2024) and Eventbrite (Mar 2026), plus Evernote/Vimeo/AOL; 2026 IPO raised $1.68B for more acquisitions. Playbook: buy stalled PMF assets cheap, cut staff, raise prices, paywall. Relevance: one landlord controls our two most important structured-event sources — the single biggest supply-side threat to our connector tier list; should be a named risk in the spec.

## Verified findings

1. **[adjusted] Usage frequency is the category's most consistently documented killer.** Founders of two failed event-discovery apps independently attest that event-seeking is a low-frequency behavior, so destination apps never form a habit and retention collapses regardless of recommendation quality. Hugecity's founder (who grew the site to ~1M visitors/month) reported that 90% of *Hugecity's users* visited once a week or less, with only ~10% "tastemaker" daily users (note: a stat about his own user base, not a population-level claim). Plancast's founder cited low sharing frequency ("plans are suitable for only occasional sharing"; users "don't develop a strong daily or weekly habit") as one of roughly ten interlocking failure causes. The frequency problem is consistent with — though not explicitly documented as the cause of — the other destination-app exits (YPlan to Time Out, Sosh acquihired by Postmates, IRL shut down 2023 after its board found 95% of its claimed 20M users were bots).

2. **[adjusted] There is little viable monetization between discovery and the ticket.** Affiliate/ads revenue for pure event aggregators has historically been thin — Malkin (2015) describes third-party ticket referrals as a "not very profitable partner kickback," with even successful discovery players (e.g., Time Out) really monetizing general-content advertising. Outcomes corroborate: Zvents (3M events, 14M monthly uniques via 300+ media partners) exited to StubHub/eBay for an undisclosed, reportedly modest sum and was shut down; YPlan raised ~£31M (~$38M) and sold for £1.6M upfront; DICE — with almost half of ticket sales driven by personalized recommendations AND owning checkout — still lost $51.1M on $28.5M revenue in 2022 and was acquired by Fever (June 2025); Eventbrite, owning inventory and checkout, went private at roughly $500M ($4.50/share, closed March 2026) versus its ~$1.76B IPO valuation. No verified public figure supports the researcher's "$12-24M/yr revenue ceiling" — the cap is real but should be stated qualitatively.

3. **[adjusted] Event supply aggregation is a structurally hostile, negative-incentive scrape war.** The richest sources go API-dark or monetize access, organizers concentrate on owned channels, and ticketing incumbents defend the transaction layer with litigation-scale force. Facebook — whose Events product had ~100M daily users at its 2016 peak — restricted the Events API in April 2018 (guest lists, wall posts, RSVP removed; access limited to approved partners), and average Page organic reach eroded across a decade from ~16% (2012, EdgeRank Checker) to ~1-2% today — the largest event corpus is effectively unreachable programmatically. Songkick's move from discovery into ticketing ended with Live Nation "effectively blocking" its US ticketing business; the suit settled Jan 2018 for $110M, with Warner Music taking the discovery brand and Live Nation acquiring the remaining IP. As of March 2026 one cost-cutting operator (Bending Spoons) owns BOTH Meetup (Jan 2024) and Eventbrite (~$505M, closed Mar 10, 2026), with a documented fee-hike/paywall playbook (Meetup organizer subscription prices tripled effective June 6, 2024 on under 30 days' notice, driving documented organizer exodus). Upcoming and Zvents show crowdsourced/syndicated supply decays without paid incentive. (Note: the widely repeated "16M events created/month" Facebook figure could not be sourced and is dropped.)

4. **[adjusted] Every survivor escaped pure consumer-facing aggregation by monetizing something beyond the discovery feed.** Fever manufactures its own inventory (Originals — Candlelight alone spans 100-150+ cities with 3M+ guests; a margin advantage is plausible but undisclosed — the "20-30% higher gross margins" figure circulating online traces only to an AI content mill and is dropped). Fever/DICE and Fatsoma own ticketing rails (Fever acquired DICE June 2025); Meetup charges organizer subscriptions; Time Out operates physical Markets; and Bandsintown — the closest thing to a surviving standalone discovery layer (independent since 2007, 100M registered users, exclusive YouTube concert-listings partner) — monetizes supply-side promotion tools (Bandsintown Pro/Marketplace), not consumer discovery. REFUTED as an absolute: "no pure discovery layer has ever survived" — Bandsintown falsifies it, but in a way that sharpens the thesis: no one has survived by charging for, or selling ads against, the consumer discovery feed itself.

5. **[unverifiable — analytical synthesis, medium confidence] Our closed-loop + calendar-native design neutralizes failure modes (b) retention death and (c) discovery monetization, but NOT (a) supply freshness/coverage cost, and only partially (d) local density.** Neutralized: an agent that works while the app is closed converts the fatal low-frequency usage pattern into a design assumption — value is delivered as confirmed calendar entries, not sessions; Songkick's artist-tracking (the one retention mechanic that worked) is the standing-subscription-to-taste pattern we generalize. Charging a subscription for LABOR (search+register+reconcile) escapes the affiliate-crumbs trap; Jukely showed ~$25/mo events willingness-to-pay exists. Not neutralized: we still bear the scrape war — worse now that Bending Spoons owns Meetup+Eventbrite and Facebook is dark; per-metro coverage/freshness is a real recurring cost, mitigated but not eliminated by acting via the user's own authenticated accounts (per-user agency is harder to lock out than central scraping). Density: an agent can only fill Friday night if matching supply exists in the user's metro; a thin result-set kills trust in week one — though we need supply density only, not user-network density.

6. **[high confidence, researcher-tagged] Geographic expansion killed more of these companies than product quality did.** Event value decays with distance, supply-curation cost is per-city, demand is spiky (roughly half of tickets at announce, half week-of), so multi-city scaling multiplies burn without network effects. YPlan (layoffs after expansion, sold for scraps), Sosh (ran out of money expanding beyond SF), Jukely (stalled after its ~20-city push), WillCall (loved but capped at 3 cities by per-venue supply deals). Fever is the exception that proves it: its Originals FORMAT replicated across cities, not curated third-party supply.

7. **[high confidence, researcher-tagged] The 2024-2026 consolidation cuts both ways.** Bending Spoons (Meetup Jan 2024 + Eventbrite Mar 2026), Fever (DICE 2025), Suno (Songkick Nov 2025): our two most important RSVP-capable sources are owned by an operator with a fee-hike/paywall/API-monetization playbook, and organizer flight is accelerating supply fragmentation toward Luma, Partiful, and Discord. Connector risk is up, but the fragmentation is exactly what makes a cross-source concierge valuable — no single destination can claim coverage anymore.

8. **[medium confidence, researcher-tagged] The Facebook incumbent-shadow argument against entering this category is weaker in 2026 than at any point in its history.** Malkin (2015) argued Facebook could take the market whenever it wanted while 50+ startups A/B-tested for it. By 2022-2025 that threat inverted: API closure, ~1-2% organic reach, organizer exodus, LinkedIn overtaking Facebook for event promotion. For the first time since ~2010 there is no dominant default event-discovery surface.

## Product implications

1. **Price the labor, not the discovery.** Charge a concierge subscription for search+register+reconcile work performed. Every affiliate/ads-monetized aggregator capped out or died; Jukely proved ~$25/mo events willingness-to-pay exists. Anchor pricing on "hours saved + events actually attended," never on listings.
2. **Success metric = confirmed calendar events per user per month (and attended-rate), explicitly NOT DAU/sessions.** The category's low usage frequency must be the design assumption: the product must deliver full value in zero-open weeks (proactive digest, auto-RSVP, calendar writes). Any roadmap item justified by "increases app opens" is recreating the graveyard.
3. **No destination feed/app as the primary surface.** The calendar + chat/notification channel IS the product surface; a browsable feed is at most a settings/override view. Songkick's artist-tracking (standing subscription to taste) is the one retention mechanic that ever worked — our persistent NL preference profile generalizes it and is the core asset.
4. **Add a named supply-risk register with Bending Spoons as risk #1** (owner of both Meetup and Eventbrite since Mar 2026): expect API fee hikes, paywalls, tightened ToS. Mitigations: per-user authenticated access as the default posture (user's own accounts are harder to lock out than central scraping), no source >40% of any metro's supply, connector deprecation playbooks. Meetup connector = depreciating asset.
5. **Launch single-metro deep, not multi-city shallow.** Multi-city expansion is what actually killed YPlan/Sosh/Jukely (per-city supply cost, location decay, no cross-city network effect). Gate metro #2 on metro #1 hitting a supply-density bar (e.g., ≥N matching candidate events per user request in ≥90% of requests).
6. **Ride the fragmentation tailwind in positioning.** Facebook Events' collapse and Meetup's organizer exodus scattered supply across Luma/Partiful/Eventbrite/Discord/venue sites with no dominant discovery surface for the first time since ~2010 — "one agent across all the places events now hide" is the honest wedge. Prioritize Luma/Partiful connectors (they absorb Meetup refugees).
7. **Target the ~10% proactive event-goer prosumer segment, not mass consumers.** The mass market does not seek events often enough to sustain a consumer app; IRL faked its way to fake scale. High-frequency social planners paying $10-25/mo have honest unit economics; onboarding should qualify for event-frequency intent.
8. **Stay out of the transaction/ticketing layer at launch** (already settled as FREE-RSVP-only; the graveyard vindicates this hard): Songkick's ticketing move triggered a Live Nation kill-zone war; WillCall needed per-venue deals. One-tap human handoff keeps us outside incumbent defenses.
9. **Missing requirement: supply-density preflight + honest-failure UX.** When a metro/timeslot has thin coverage, the concierge must say so and widen constraints rather than pad with weak matches — thin-result weeks are the trust-death moment; freshness/coverage cost is the failure mode our architecture does NOT neutralize.
10. **Optional future wedge (not launch scope): squeezed organizers.** Meetup fee-tripling refugees and post-Facebook reach collapse create a motivated supply side that survivors monetized (Fatsoma, Eventbrite Ads, Bandsintown Pro). A later "get your event into concierges" feed/API could become our owned downstream asset — the Fever lesson applied without producing events ourselves.
